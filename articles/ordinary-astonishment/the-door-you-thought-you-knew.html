Ordinary Astonishment

The Door You Thought You Knew

On familiarity, déjà vu, jamais vu, and the strange distance between knowing something and feeling that we know it.

On déjà vu, jamais vu, and the curious ways familiarity shapes what we think we know.

Door.

Look at the word for a moment. You know what it means without having to think about it. Now write it down, then keep writing the same word for at least a minute.

Somewhere past the twentieth or thirtieth repetition, something odd tends to happen. The letters start to look wrong. The two o's seem excessive, almost invented, and the word begins to loosen from the object it names. You still know perfectly well what a door is, and that door is the word for one. Yet for a few seconds it can begin to resemble gobbledygook, or a message written in a language you don't recognise.

This is a documented effect rather than a private glitch. In 2020, psychologist Chris Moulin and colleagues at the universities of Grenoble and St Andrews asked volunteers to copy familiar words repeatedly until they experienced something peculiar, or simply no longer wanted to continue. One of the words was door. About two-thirds of participants reported a strange sensation on at least one trial, most often after around thirty repetitions, roughly a minute of writing the same word.

The researchers called the experience jamais vu, French for "never seen". It is often described as a counterpart to déjà vu. With déjà vu, something new, or something we cannot remember encountering before, is accompanied by a powerful sense of familiarity. Jamais vu moves in the other direction: something we know feels temporarily strange.

That sounds straightforward until familiarity itself becomes the thing under examination.

A feeling of familiarity is not necessarily mistaken. You may recognise someone in a crowd without having any idea where you know them from, only to remember later that you worked together years ago.

At other times the feeling is harder to account for. Déjà vu can produce an intense sense that a present moment has happened before, even when no memory can be found to explain the feeling. Science can investigate when déjà vu occurs, what happens in memory and recognition, and which neurological processes accompany it. Human beings have nevertheless attached many other explanations to the experience, including reincarnation, precognition and unconventional ideas about memory. The experience of déjà vu does not, by itself, provide evidence for reincarnation, precognition or any other metaphysical explanation.

Even so, I am reluctant to treat the limits of scientific explanation as the point at which curiosity must stop. Science can describe mechanisms and patterns without necessarily settling every philosophical question we attach to the experience.

Jamais vu creates a different disturbance. Here the familiar itself becomes strange.

And words are not the only things capable of doing that.

The stranger in the mirror

A different experiment involves something considerably more intimate than a word: your own face.

In 2010, psychologist Giovanni Caputo reported what he called the strange-face-in-the-mirror illusion. Participants sat in a dimly lit room and gazed at their own reflected faces. Within about a minute, many began seeing distortions. After prolonged gazing, some reported an unfamiliar face, the altered face of somebody they knew, an animal, or other startling transformations.

This is not jamais vu. The perceptual mechanisms being studied are different. What makes the experiment relevant here is the object undergoing the transformation.

Your own face should surely rank among the most familiar things you possess, yet even that familiarity is less stable than it seems.

There is a peculiar quality to looking directly into your own eyes for long enough, even outside an experiment. Most of the time we glance at a mirror for practical reasons and move on. If you remain there and keep looking into your own eyes, the familiarity of the face can begin to loosen. Instead of simply recognising yourself, you may become acutely aware that you are looking at a face at all, one that is both intimately yours and, for a moment, oddly separate from the person doing the looking.

Who exactly is this person?

Caputo's research cannot answer that question. It belongs somewhere else, closer to philosophy and the strange problem of being both the person who experiences the world and something within the world that can be looked at.

What interests me is how little it sometimes takes to put distance between us and something we thought could never become unfamiliar.

Familiarity

We talk about familiarity as though it were one of the qualities an object possesses. A familiar face. A familiar house. A familiar song.

Of course, objects and places can contain details that genuinely help us recognise them. A childhood home may still have the crack made by a badly aimed ball, an uneven step you tripped over repeatedly, a mark in a wall, or an object built by someone you loved. Those details belong to the place and may identify it immediately.

The feeling accompanying recognition depends on more than the object itself.

A recording of a song can remain unchanged for decades while its associations accumulate. A song linked to someone we loved may continue to carry the warmth of that memory even after death, separation, or time has added grief, regret, or distance to it. The music remains the same; what it evokes can become more complicated.

That is part of what makes familiarity difficult to pin down. Previous encounters matter, but so do memory, association and whatever has happened since.

Recognition can also behave in ways that expose processes we normally experience as one seamless act. Studies of people with Capgras delusion have shown that someone may consciously recognise a familiar face while failing to show the usual autonomic response associated with seeing someone known to them. The person may know exactly who they are looking at while something about the expected feeling of familiarity is disturbed.

Familiarity, recognition and memory overlap so thoroughly in everyday life that we rarely notice the distinctions among them. What interests researchers are the moments when those processes no longer line up in the usual way.

A familiar face may remain familiar while the name refuses to arrive. A feeling of recognition may appear first and turn out to have been accurate. Déjà vu can produce familiarity without a retrievable memory to explain it.

The more closely you examine it, the less neatly the pieces arrange themselves.

The Penumbra of Knowing

There are things you almost certainly believe but cannot remember how you came to believe them. There are things we know without remembering when we first came to know them. Trying to give examples is strangely difficult, because the moment I choose one, somebody else may be able to locate it precisely in their own history. Perhaps I could too, if I thought about it for long enough.

Then there are assumptions whose histories are harder to reconstruct. Ideas about other people, about events in the past, about what is normal or threatening, respectable or shameful, may have reached us through family, school, culture, politics, religion, media, experience, or some mixture we could no longer untangle if we tried. Some survive close examination. Others do not.

Repetition can complicate matters further because a claim heard often enough may eventually acquire the feeling of something long known.

Of course.

It is an interesting phrase: it can mean the evidence is overwhelming, or mark the point at which we have stopped asking where the belief came from.

Psychologists have studied one mechanism that may contribute to this. The illusory truth effect describes the tendency for repeated statements to be judged as more truthful than unfamiliar ones. The effect has limits. Prior knowledge, plausibility and context matter, and people do not simply believe everything they hear repeatedly. Even so, repetition can influence judgements of truth.

One explanation involves processing fluency. Information encountered before is often easier for the mind to process, and that ease can contribute to a sense that the information is credible.

The room with a thousand doors

A claim appears on your phone and disappears with the next swipe. Later you hear an echo of it in a video. You encounter it again in a screenshot, then in commentary about the screenshot, and eventually somebody you trust shares another version of the same story.

After a while, it can become difficult to remember how many genuinely independent sources you have encountered. What feels like corroboration may sometimes be the same claim travelling through different people, platforms and contexts.

Studies of repeated misinformation give us reason to take this seriously. Encountering a false claim more than once can increase its perceived accuracy and, under some conditions, people's willingness to share it.

The internet did not invent selection or sensationalism. Newspapers, broadcasters and other forms of media have always decided what becomes a story, which angle leads, whose voice appears, which image is chosen and what is left outside the frame. Digital media adds a further layer. Algorithms can influence which fragments we encounter repeatedly, while social platforms have commercial reasons to value attention, engagement and return visits.

An idea can therefore become familiar for reasons that have very little to do with its importance or truth.

The same technologies have also given us extraordinary access to lives unfolding far beyond our own immediate world. Someone living thousands of kilometres away no longer has to exist for us only as a name on a map or as whatever our own culture has told us about them.

What reaches us may be entirely real without being representative. Every photograph, story or recording captures a particular person or event from a particular point of view and at a particular moment. Repeated exposure to a narrow selection of those moments can nevertheless begin to shape what we think we know about the whole.

My own feeds rarely offer an uncomplicated view of ordinary human life elsewhere. I see war, grief, political fury, suffering and division. I see people becoming increasingly convinced of the depravity of whichever group stands opposite them. Occasionally a story of cooperation, discovery or ordinary human decency appears, and its rarity is almost part of what makes me notice it. More than once I have gone looking for such a story in mainstream coverage and wondered why information that widened what I could see of the situation was so difficult to find.

That does not mean hopeful stories are deliberately being hidden, nor that social media offers some purer reality. It means that every view of the world reaches us through acts of selection.

Perhaps the question is not only how much of the world we can see, but what keeps being placed before us.

When strangers acquire faces

We are living through a peculiar contradiction. Fragments of individual human experience are available to us on a scale previous generations could scarcely have imagined, while the technologies carrying those fragments can also intensify stereotypes and division.

A nationality can still be discussed as though millions of people possess one mind. A religion can be reduced to whatever its most frightening representatives said this week. Political opponents can be reduced to a collection of their worst examples.

Individual lives rarely fit as neatly into the versions of them that reach us.

Research into mediated intergroup contact suggests that encounters with people perceived as belonging to another group can, under some circumstances, reduce prejudice. There is something hopeful in that finding, but exposure has no single direction. If most of what reaches us from a place is war, extremism, political conflict, or suffering, those images may form our most familiar version of that place. We can see more of one another than any previous generation and still know one another through a narrow selection of moments.

Research on intergroup contact does not suggest that encountering another person reliably dissolves prejudice. Clearly it does not. But stereotypes can become harder to preserve unchanged when experience keeps supplying individuals who fail to behave according to them.

Someone who had existed only as one of those people begins to emerge as an individual whose life does not fit comfortably inside the generalisation. The generalisation may survive, but it has been disturbed.

And the question of recognising another life does not necessarily stop with our own species.

Elephants have been observed showing sustained interest in the bodies and bones of dead members of their species. In 2018, an orca known as Tahlequah carried her dead calf for at least seventeen days and roughly a thousand miles.

Scientists are appropriately cautious when describing the internal emotional lives of other animals. Behaviour can be observed; subjective experience cannot be directly inspected. Anthropomorphism is a legitimate concern because human beings are very capable of seeing ourselves where the evidence does not justify it.

I understand that caution. I am less certain that anthropomorphism is the only mistake available to us.

Science cannot tell us what Tahlequah experienced internally. Calling her behaviour grief is therefore an interpretation, and some researchers would resist the word for precisely that reason. I find it the most convincing interpretation, even while I accept that I cannot know what the experience felt like from inside an orca's mind.

The animal does not have to grieve exactly as I do before the possibility of grief deserves to be taken seriously.

When the writing became unfamiliar

An unexpectedly appropriate problem arose while I was working on this essay.

At points I had leaned on AI as an aid in research and editing. Somewhere during that process, the writing became extraordinarily polished. Arguments seemed to move effortlessly from one idea to the next. Metaphors appeared in exactly the right places. Paragraphs ended with satisfying conclusions. The whole piece seemed coherent enough that, at first, I hardly noticed where some of that coherence was only apparent.

Then I began looking at individual sentences.

A metaphor sounded profound until I tried to explain what it actually meant. Ideas sat beside one another because they produced a satisfying rhythm rather than because they belonged to a defensible category. A conclusion could arrive elegantly from a premise that had never properly been established.

Some of the writing was beautiful.

Some of it was beautifully wrong.

There was an irony in discovering this while writing about familiarity. I had become familiar with the essay as a finished object, and its fluency had helped persuade me that I understood what it was saying.

Then I asked AI to make the prose sound more human, which made the experiment more disorienting still. The result often sounded less like me. Some passages became painfully plain. In others, the language remained plausible while the reasoning became weaker, as though the surface had survived after parts of the structure underneath it had been removed.

Eventually I stopped asking how human the prose sounded and began reading it slowly enough to ask a more useful question: did I actually believe what each sentence was saying?

That meant checking whether the metaphors made sense, whether the examples supported the argument, whether supposedly related ideas really belonged together, and whether I would stand behind the sentence if somebody asked me to explain it.

There is an unsettling resonance in the word hallucination, which is also used for certain failures of generative AI. The term is metaphorical: a human hallucination is a sensory experience, while a language model has no subjective experience to hallucinate. Yet the borrowed word catches at a resemblance. Generative AI can produce false or unsupported information with extraordinary fluency, giving coherence to material that does not correspond to reality.

A piece of writing can carry every outward signal of coherence and still be wrong, and this is hardly unique to machines. People are drawn to patterns, categories, symmetry and explanations. We use analogy and metaphor, and we like arguments that resolve. We are capable of forcing unruly reality into shapes that are easier to understand, and sometimes easier to tolerate. Large language models did not invent those habits. They learned them from us.

Research into whether readers can tell human and AI writing apart makes the boundary harder to locate. In a 2026 study published in Judgment and Decision Making, participants trying to distinguish human-written stories from AI-generated ones performed at or below chance. In one experiment, 39.39 percent correctly identified which story had been written by a human and which by AI; in another, performance was essentially at chance.

Other research suggests that people may rate creative work differently once they believe AI produced or assisted it, even when the words themselves have not changed.

Our judgement of a piece can therefore shift when its presumed origin changes.

Once I became familiar with the rhetorical habits I associated with AI, I began finding them everywhere, including in unquestionably human writing and in my own habits from long before I ever used a language model.

I love metaphor and rhythm, and I like returning to an image at the end of an essay. Those things do not belong to machines. What I had to relearn was the difference between language that gives an idea shape and language that hides the fact that the idea has none.

Looking again

There is a larger problem underneath all of this.

The world contains far more information than any mind could examine individually. We survive partly because perception and memory are selective. We recognise patterns, group things together, anticipate what is likely to happen and allow enormous amounts of familiar information to remain in the background. Usually this works so well that we never notice it happening.

Neurological illnesses make the importance of these processes painfully visible. People living with dementia, for example, can lose familiarity with places, objects or people that once required no conscious effort to recognise. What appears effortless in ordinary life depends upon cognitive systems whose work becomes obvious when they fail.

The same ability to simplify the world also creates vulnerabilities. Categories that help us navigate experience can harden into stereotypes, while repetition can lend credibility to an idea that has not earned it. Even language itself can mislead us when fluency makes weak reasoning seem stronger than it is.

We often recognise enough of the shape to assume we understand the whole. Living in permanent suspicion of everything we perceive would be impossible. We need familiarity and assumptions that usually work; we need to let most doors remain doors without stopping to interrogate the metaphysics of the handle every time we leave the house. Occasionally, though, we are given reason to look again.

A historical figure becomes more complicated when we learn a detail omitted from the version we inherited. A family story changes after another person tells what happened from where they were standing. An old belief begins to look different when we finally ask where it came from.

The disruption can also be smaller and stranger: a familiar street suddenly looks foreign, your own face in a mirror acquires distance, or a word written thirty times begins to look as though it belongs to another language. For a moment, the usual recognition falters, and what we thought we knew becomes available for inspection again.

Door

Look at the word once more.

Door.

It probably looks ordinary again.

A door is one of the simplest pieces of architecture imaginable, yet human cultures have repeatedly given thresholds meanings far beyond their practical function. The Romans had Janus, the two-faced god associated with gates, beginnings, and transitions. Across cultures, doorways have been surrounded by rituals involving entry and departure. In The Book of Symbols, the gate appears repeatedly as a boundary between states: inside and outside, known and unknown, one condition of being and whatever comes after it.

Stand on one side of a door, and you are here. Cross it, and you are somewhere else.

None of that requires a door to be any particular thing. A door does not always have hinges, or even close. Sometimes it is simply an opening left in a wall. And still, in one form or another, people have treated it as the boundary between one life and another.

The experiment at the beginning of this essay makes a much smaller crossing. Through repetition, a familiar word temporarily loses the feeling that normally accompanies it. For a moment, we have to look at something we usually pass through without noticing.

I began with a word and eventually found the same question appearing in memory, belief, other people, other animals, technology, and the language I was using to think about all of them.

None of that requires us to distrust the familiar simply because it is familiar. It asks something less dramatic and perhaps more useful: that every so often we remain capable of seeing it again.

Door.

A door is still a door.

Until you look at it for long enough.

Then, sometimes, it becomes an opening.
