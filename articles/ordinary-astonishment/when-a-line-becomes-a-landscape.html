Ordinary Astonishment

When a Line Becomes a Landscape

How decisions drawn on maps become railways, neighbourhoods, walls and borders, and continue shaping lives long after the people who drew them are gone.

At the border between Finland and Sweden, Europe changes width by 89 millimetres.

On the Finnish side, the rails are 1,524 millimetres apart. On the Swedish side, as across most of Europe, they are 1,435. The difference is small enough to span with a hand, but large enough that an ordinary train cannot simply continue from one network to the other. Finland's wider gauge has its origins in the period when the country was part of the Russian Empire. Finland became independent in 1917, but the measurement survived the empire that had helped determine it. Today the two gauges actually extend across the border: Finnish 1,524 mm trains can travel as far as Haparanda in Sweden, while Swedish 1,435 mm trains can reach Tornio in Finland.

On 10 August 2026, passenger trains began travelling from Finland into Haparanda again. Political Europe has changed almost beyond recognition since those railway systems developed. Finland and Sweden now belong to the European Union, the Schengen Area and NATO. At the railway station, however, history is still 89 millimetres wide.

It is an unusually literal example of something we ordinarily overlook. Maps are covered in lines that appear to describe the world: borders, municipal limits, property boundaries, railway routes, administrative regions. Yet the relationship between the line and the world beneath it is stranger than that. Sometimes a line records a difference that already exists. Sometimes people draw the line first and spend decades making the landscape conform to it.

Sometimes the distortion lies not in where the lines are drawn, but in what happens to the world inside them. For centuries, one of the most familiar ways of depicting the globe has been the Mercator projection, devised in 1569 for navigation. Its usefulness came with a mathematical trade-off: preserving angles and compass directions on a flat surface increasingly exaggerates area towards the poles. Greenland can consequently appear comparable in size to Africa, although Africa is about fourteen times larger. Nothing about Africa's physical extent changed. What changed was the convention used to represent it.

In February 2026, the African Union endorsed an initiative encouraging the use of projections that more accurately represent the relative size of Africa, including Equal Earth. In September, the United Nations General Assembly voted by 164 votes to one, with six abstentions, for a resolution encouraging governments, schools, international organisations and technology companies to use Equal Earth and other equal-area projections when relative size matters. It did not ban Mercator, nor did it establish a perfectly undistorted map: no flat representation of a spherical planet can preserve area, shape, distance and direction simultaneously. Instead, it drew attention to something maps easily allow us to forget. Every projection makes choices about what it will preserve and what it will distort, and those choices help determine the world we become accustomed to seeing.

What makes a line real?

A child with a colouring book encounters the problem in its simplest form. The printed boundary has no physical power over the crayon. Wax will cross ink without resistance. Staying inside the line requires agreement, obedience, or somebody standing nearby insisting upon it.

Political lines begin almost as helplessly. Ink cannot stop a train or a person. It cannot separate neighbours, determine who owns a field or make one language end where another begins. For that, the line needs assistance from law, infrastructure, habit, money, fences, deeds, roads, courts, checkpoints and, sometimes, soldiers. Given enough of these, an abstraction on a map can become a physical fact on the ground.

Cape Town offers an uncomfortable example. Under apartheid, racial classification was translated deliberately into urban geography. The Group Areas Board proposed using railway lines to demarcate white areas from Black and Coloured ones. In 1954, areas including Pinelands, Thornton, Epping and Milnerton north of the railway were zoned white, and the railway became an important racial boundary. Elsewhere, roads, industrial belts and stretches of open land could perform similar work, separating communities that the state had classified differently. Forced removals supplied the violence necessary to make those classifications real.

The legislation eventually disappeared, but the city did not rearrange itself when it did. Railways still follow their tracks. Roads continue along the routes built for them, and houses remain where houses were permitted to be built. Cape Town has subsequently tried to reconnect parts of this inherited geography through public transport, new transport corridors and other forms of urban planning. MyCiTi's Phase 2A, for example, is being built to connect more than 30 communities from Khayelitsha and Mitchells Plain to Wynberg and Claremont, with the City explicitly describing spatial integration as one of its aims. Yet altering the physical structure of a city is a much slower undertaking than changing the law that helped create it. A political idea can therefore survive its own repeal, not as an argument anybody still needs to make, but in the distances people must travel between home and work.

That history complicates the meaning of boundaries being built in Cape Town now. In 2026 the City announced R114 million for its N2 Edge Safety Project, including a concrete barrier along roughly eight kilometres of the freeway. The City presents the project as a response to crime and a means of protecting motorists and communities along the route. Ndithini Tyhido, the ANC's leader of the opposition in the City Council, has argued that it will also sanitise what visitors see while travelling from the airport, screening poorer communities from view. That accusation concerns motive and is contested, but the disagreement itself is revealing. A new wall does not arrive without context. It inherits the history of the place in which it is built, alongside the intentions of its designers and the interpretations of those who will live beside it. The same concrete can therefore be understood as protection, exclusion or concealment depending upon where a person is standing.

Berlin once demonstrated the process more brutally. On 13 August 1961, the boundary between East and West Berlin began turning physical. Streets were blocked, crossings closed and barriers erected. Wire was followed by increasingly elaborate systems of concrete walls, patrol roads, watchtowers and border installations.

At Sebastianstraße, geography became almost absurdly precise. The Ohlendorf family lived at number 84 in West Berlin, but the street and pavement immediately outside their house belonged to East Berlin. The first barriers left the pavement before the West Berlin houses free, while concrete blocks were erected along the street. A geopolitical division had arrived quite literally at the front door.

When the political order sustaining the Wall collapsed in 1989, its physical authority collapsed with it. People crossed, new openings appeared, border installations were dismantled and pieces of concrete became souvenirs. Berlin gives the line a peculiar life cycle: an idea becomes wire, then concrete, then an engineered landscape; once the institutions maintaining it disappear, what seemed immovable can become rubble.

The reverse is also possible: a people can exist without a sovereign boundary enclosing them. Around 30 million Kurds live across a region principally divided among Turkey, Iraq, Iran and Syria. Kurdish communities are themselves politically, linguistically and religiously diverse, but the Kurds remain one of the world's largest peoples without a sovereign state of their own.

For a brief moment after the First World War, the map might have developed differently. Articles 62 to 64 of the 1920 Treaty of Sèvres provided for a scheme of local autonomy in predominantly Kurdish areas and, under specified conditions, opened a possible route towards independence. The settlement did not take effect as envisaged and was superseded by the Treaty of Lausanne in 1923, which contained no equivalent provision for a Kurdish state. A century later, Kurdish populations remain distributed across several internationally recognised states, while the Kurdistan Region of Iraq has constitutional autonomy within Iraq.

Look at a conventional political map and this is easy to miss. Turkey has an outline. Iraq has an outline. Iran and Syria have outlines. Each can be given a different colour, while Kurdish human geography passes through those colours. The map suggests distinctions that human geography refuses to observe so neatly.

Few places expose the consequences of that discrepancy more painfully than Palestine. Here it is especially important not to imagine a single border steadily moving across an otherwise static map, because the lines themselves have changed in meaning.

In 1947, the United Nations proposed partitioning Mandatory Palestine into Arab and Jewish states. War followed the end of the British Mandate and the establishment of Israel in 1948, accompanied by the mass displacement of Palestinians. The armistice agreements of 1949 produced demarcation lines that were explicitly not intended as final political or territorial boundaries. Yet they became the practical divisions of a new territorial reality: the West Bank came under Jordanian control and Gaza under Egyptian administration.

Those arrangements lasted until another war altered the geography in June 1967. After weeks of escalating regional tensions, including Egypt's closure of the Straits of Tiran to Israeli shipping and military mobilisation in Sinai, Israel launched a pre-emptive attack against Egypt on 5 June. Jordan and Syria were drawn into the fighting, and over the following six days Israel captured the Gaza Strip and Sinai Peninsula from Egypt, the West Bank and East Jerusalem from Jordan, and the Golan Heights from Syria. The war dramatically changed the territory under Israeli control and began the Israeli occupation of the Palestinian territories.

What followed over subsequent decades was not simply another line but an increasingly complicated geography of occupation, settlements, military areas, roads, checkpoints, permits and different administrative jurisdictions.

The Green Line itself illustrates the peculiar authority of cartography. What began as the armistice demarcation of 1949 became one of the central geographical references in the conflict, even though the agreements had expressly denied those lines the status of permanent political borders.

In the West Bank, another structure later complicated that geography further. Israel began constructing a barrier in 2002, stating that its purpose was to prevent attacks inside Israel. In 2026, OCHA described the barrier as approximately 712 kilometres long, 64 per cent built, with about 85 per cent of its route running inside the West Bank rather than along the Green Line. Depending on location, it consists of combinations of fencing, ditches, razor wire, patrol roads, monitoring systems and high concrete wall sections. Together with its gates and permit system, OCHA describes it as the single largest obstacle to Palestinian movement within the West Bank.

Here the relationship between map and landscape becomes difficult to separate. There is the armistice line, the barrier, settlement boundaries, roads, military restrictions and the different administrative arrangements produced by the Oslo process. A person may be geographically close to a field, workplace, school, hospital or relative and nevertheless inhabit a very different geography of access.

Gaza produces another variation. Its boundaries emerged from the war of 1948 and the 1949 Egyptian-Israeli armistice agreement. Israel occupied the Strip in 1967 and in 2005 removed its settlements and withdrew its permanent military presence from inside Gaza, while retaining control over areas including its airspace, maritime access and much of the external perimeter. After Hamas took control of Gaza in 2007, Israel sharply intensified restrictions on the movement of people and goods, citing security concerns; Egypt also restricted passage across its border.

Restrictions above ground helped produce activity below it. Tunnels beneath the Gaza-Egypt border carried construction materials, livestock, fuel, cash, food and other goods that were difficult or impossible to obtain through ordinary crossings, while tunnels were also used to move weapons and people. At their height they supported a substantial informal economy. Measures intended to control movement across the surface therefore produced routes beneath it, which in turn generated further efforts to detect, destroy or prevent underground passage. The political boundary had become three-dimensional, contested not only across the ground but through the earth beneath it.

The history since October 2023 makes any purely abstract discussion of Gaza inadequate. The Hamas-led attacks of 7 October killed about 1,200 people in Israel and were followed by a prolonged Israeli military campaign that devastated Gaza. Tens of thousands of Palestinians have been killed, most of the population has been displaced, and homes and civilian infrastructure have been destroyed on an enormous scale. The lines on maps cannot be separated from the people required to live within their physical consequences.

The ceasefire announced in October 2025 produced another kind of boundary. A newly introduced “Yellow Line” indicated the area in which Israeli troops would remain temporarily deployed, initially spanning about 53 per cent of the Gaza Strip. In practice, according to OCHA, it came to mark roughly where access restrictions were enforced on most Palestinians. Israeli authorities also communicated a separate “Orange Line”, extending farther into populated areas and requiring humanitarian organisations to coordinate movement within the larger restricted area. By June 2026, after the line had been shifted several times, OCHA calculated that 64.9 per cent of Gaza lay within this area of tighter and more direct Israeli control. These are not internationally recognised borders, and their position is not always obvious to the people living beside them: yellow concrete blocks have sometimes been placed away from the mapped Yellow Line, while residents have described learning where access is restricted from gunfire and the disappearance of services. Here a line need not even become a continuous wall to acquire physical force. Its reality emerges from what happens when somebody crosses it.

Yet people repeatedly prove less obedient to maps than maps imply. Families cross borders; languages spread across them; economies find routes around restrictions; people migrate, return, commute, trade, marry and remember places from which they have been separated. Kurdish identity persists without a sovereign Kurdish outline. Palestinian identity persists through displacement, occupation, fragmentation and war. An imperial railway measurement persists after the empire that helped establish it. A Cape Town railway continues through a city from which the racial legislation that once gave it additional political meaning has been abolished. Berlin demonstrates the reverse: even concrete can lose its authority when the political system sustaining it disappears.

None of this makes borders unreal. On the contrary, a sufficiently enforced human invention can become one of the most consequential facts in a person's life. It can determine which passport they carry, whether they can enter a road, where a train stops, which school they attend, whether they can reach a field, what jurisdiction governs their house and whether crossing a few metres of ground is an ordinary walk or a criminal offence.

The line itself, however, is never doing the work. Behind it are agreements, institutions, histories, technologies and people willing or compelled to maintain it. Remove those and some lines fade astonishingly quickly. Leave their physical machinery behind and others survive the political worlds that created them.

Perhaps that is the strangest thing about looking at a map. Its lines appear to describe a world already there, when sometimes they function more like instructions, followed by generations of labour, money, concrete, steel and human lives spent trying to make the ground obey them.
