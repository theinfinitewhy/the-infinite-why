Proof of Hope

The girl on the riverbed

How one teenager's answer to an impossible question grew into a home, a school, and a community that now cares for its own.

9 August 2026

We tend to imagine that lasting change begins with something large. A grant. A government programme. A plan drawn up by people with the resources to carry it out.

But this particular story, one that has since given a home to nearly ninety children and a free education to hundreds more, began with something far smaller than that.

One girl, breaking stones by a river.

The girl with the big brown eyes

In 2006, Maggie Doyne finished high school in New Jersey while her friends were preparing for university. Instead, she packed a backpack and left home.

Her travels eventually took her to Surkhet, in the foothills of Nepal's far west, a country still finding its feet after a decade of civil war. There she met a seven-year-old girl named Hima, who was breaking stones on a dry riverbed and selling them to help feed her family.

Maggie did not simply carry the image of Hima away with her. When she learned how little it would cost to put Hima into school, she paid the tuition, bought her uniform and books, and enrolled her. One child led to another, and then another.

Soon the simplicity of that first solution ran into a harder truth. Some children needed far more than school fees. Poverty, displacement and family circumstance meant that some of them needed somewhere safe to live, and an eighteen-year-old travelling alone through Nepal had no idea how to build that on her own.

She was not, however, trying to work out what came next alone. Another person had already entered the story, carrying experience Maggie did not have.

The man who came home

Maggie had met Tope Bahadur Malla at Ramana's Garden, a home in India caring for Nepali refugee children.

Tope understood this world from a very different direction. He had grown up in a small Himalayan village and been orphaned as a boy. He spent more than fifteen years caring for children at Ramana's Garden before making the decision to return to the country the war had forced him to leave.

Where Maggie brought an almost startling willingness to act, Tope brought years of experience, a deep knowledge of Nepal, and an understanding of what it actually takes to keep a vulnerable child safe.

Their different strengths became a partnership. In 2007, Maggie spent her life savings, $5,000, on a small piece of land in Surkhet. But the image of a young woman arriving with $5,000 and single-handedly building an orphanage misses what happened next, because Maggie and Tope didn't build Kopila Valley on their own. What followed was a collective effort: local families helped pour the foundations. Supporters thousands of kilometres away sent what they could. A Nepali NGO and a US nonprofit called BlinkNow were established to carry the work forward. Brick by brick, something that had begun beside a riverbed acquired walls and a roof, and in 2008, the Kopila Valley Children's Home opened its doors.

That might have been where the story ended, but the needs around them did not end at the front door of a children's home.

A home wasn't enough

Once you begin looking closely at why a child is struggling, it becomes difficult to stop at the child. There is a family behind them trying to survive. A school that may or may not be within reach. An income that rarely stretches far enough. A body that needs a doctor sometimes, and a mind that needs looking after too. A mother or sister with little say in her own future. A whole environment that helps determine whether any of it can hold.

Kopila Valley grew outward to meet all of it. A school followed. Then programmes supporting women. Health services. Counselling. Economic opportunities. Environmental initiatives, because a community's future depends on the land it stands on too.

What began as a children's home gradually expanded into a network of services rooted in the wider community.

Today, BlinkNow describes its mission not as rescuing children but as empowering Nepal's youth, in partnership with the local community, to reduce poverty, support women, improve health and advance sustainability and social justice. Its team of teachers, carers, cooks, builders and administrators is drawn overwhelmingly from that same community, and a Nepali board of leaders and elders helps govern the organisation.

That distinction matters. There is an uncomfortable version of this story that would be very easy to tell: a young American woman travels to a poorer country, encounters suffering, and saves children. It is also the least interesting version.

Nearly twenty years on, Kopila Valley exists because the project grew larger than the person who started it. The original impulse mattered enormously. But so did everything that happened to it afterwards.

In other words, the work became shared: locally staffed, locally governed and increasingly shaped by the people whose lives were bound up with it.

What one small act became

There is something startling about placing the beginning and the present side by side: one seven-year-old girl breaking stones beside a river. One teenager wondering whether she could get her into school. And, nearly twenty years later, a children's home, a school, women's programmes, health and counselling services, jobs for a community, and a generation of young Nepalis who grew up there and are now doctors, teachers and carers in their own right, some of them now looking after the Kopilas who came after them.

None of that was contained in the first act. Maggie didn't pay Hima's school fees because she knew where it would lead. She couldn't have known. She simply encountered something within reach, and did the thing that was possible then.

Why this story matters

What moves me most about Kopila Valley is not only what it became, but how it became possible. Maggie and Tope did not begin with the question how do I fix this? They kept returning to something smaller and more answerable: what is the next thing I can actually do from here? That difference matters. A promise to one child did not contain a blueprint for everything that followed; each answer exposed another need and another possible action.

A thought for today

I first came across this story years ago, when her name meant nothing to anyone I mentioned it to. Since then it has found its way into a memoir and a documentary, and reached far more people than it had when I stumbled on it. Watching that happen feels like its own small proof of the thing this story is about.

We are constantly confronted with problems whose scale exceeds us. Climate change. War. Poverty. Displacement. Inequality.

It is easy to mistake our inability to solve the whole problem for an inability to affect any part of it.

Kopila Valley suggests something gentler. You do not need to see the road ahead before taking the first step. Sometimes you help one child, and then you discover the child needs a home, and building the home introduces you to a community, and the community teaches you what it actually needs. Somewhere along the way, the thing stops belonging to you.

Hima could not have known what would follow when a stranger crossed her path on that riverbed. Neither could Maggie or Tope. They could only see the next thing that needed doing, so they did it. And then other people did the next thing, and the next, until one small decision became a place in which thousands of other decisions became possible.

Hope deserves evidence. This is some.
